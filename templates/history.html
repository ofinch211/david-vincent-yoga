THE ROOTS OF THE PRACTICE

An ancient tradition.
A living practice.

To practise yoga is to join an ongoing conversation — one with deep roots in India, and many paths.

A HISTORY WITH MANY THREADS

Beyond
the postures.

Yoga has never been just one practice, one text or one story.

Its history runs through Indian philosophical, religious and ascetic traditions, including Hindu, Buddhist and Jain settings. Meanings and methods have changed over centuries. Dates and interpretations are often debated; a single uninterrupted origin story cannot capture that richness.

A SHORT JOURNEY THROUGH TIME
ANCIENT INDIA

Early ideas & inner exploration

The Vedas form part of the early religious and philosophical background. Later Upanishads explore self, consciousness and liberation, with some discussing breath, meditation and the control of the senses. Yoga developed through this wider environment, rather than beginning as a modern exercise system.

CLASSICAL YOGA

Patanjali & the Yoga Sutras

The Yoga Sutras, attributed to Patanjali and generally placed in the early centuries CE, systematise a path of mental discipline and liberation. Their eight-limbed framework places ethical conduct, meditation and concentration alongside posture and breath.

MEDIEVAL TRADITIONS

The development of Hatha Yoga

Hatha traditions developed distinctive methods involving the body and breath, including postures, breath regulation, seals and energetic practices. Texts and communities differed in their methods and aims; Hatha was more than a label for a gentle physical class.

19TH & 20TH CENTURIES

A changing physical practice

Modern yoga emerged through exchanges between Indian traditions, reform movements and physical cultures. Tirumalai Krishnamacharya became an influential teacher of postural yoga in the 20th century. His students developed distinct approaches that reached international audiences.

YOGA TODAY

Many approaches, shared curiosity

People around the world engage with yoga through movement, philosophy, breath and meditation. Contemporary classes vary widely. Exploring the context helps us appreciate the Indian roots of the practices we encounter today.
